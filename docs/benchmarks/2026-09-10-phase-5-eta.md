# Phase 5 — pickup ETA, learned from the pickups the fleet made

**Date** 2026-09-10 · Apple silicon, 10 cores, 16 GB · infrastructure on OrbStack,
capped at 4 CPUs and 4 GB
**Under test** simulated drivers doing trips → gateway → `loc.ping` → **ingest
(pickup detection)** → `pickups.observed` → **gateway (ETA model)** → the rider
following their driver

A rider waiting on the kerb wants one number: how long until the car is here.
This predicts it from pickups the fleet has actually made rather than from an
assumed speed, and measures itself against that assumed speed on every pickup.

## Observing pickups

Ingest reads every driver's pings in order, so it sees both ends of a pickup
without being told about trips at all: the first `enroute_pickup` ping is the
driver setting off, the next `on_trip` ping is the rider getting in. The time
between them, by the driver's own clock, and the straight-line distance between
the two positions, go to `pickups.observed`, kept for a day.

Two things that measurement carries with it:

- **Up to a ping interval at each end.** A status change is seen at the next
  ping, four seconds apart, so every pickup carries some overhead that has
  nothing to do with distance.
- **A real driver reports `on_trip` at the start of the trip, not on arrival.**
  The web driver's status follows the trip, so for real drivers this measures
  pickup plus the wait for the rider. Simulated drivers start the moment they
  arrive.

The simulator had to learn to do trips for any of this to exist
(`SIM_TRIP_KM`): before, a driver who accepted went straight back to cruising,
and there was never a pickup to observe.

## The model, chosen by the data

The gateway learns from every pickup — each instance reads the topic from the
start, so one that starts mid-afternoon already knows the morning — and every
pickup is scored before it is learned from, beside a naive estimate of 30 km/h
in a straight line.

The first two models lost to that naive estimate:

| model                                   | pickups | learned error | naive error |
| --------------------------------------- | ------: | ------------: | ----------: |
| pace per cell                           |      32 |     mean 30 s |   mean 25 s |
| least-squares overhead + pace, per cell |     106 |     mean 65 s |   mean 51 s |

So the next one was chosen by replaying the real pickups — 183 of them from a
live run, in order, each predicted before it was learned from:

| model                      | mean error | median error | p90 error |
| -------------------------- | ---------: | -----------: | --------: |
| **overhead + median pace** | **65.5 s** |       38.3 s | **179 s** |
| median pace                |     65.9 s |       36.0 s |     184 s |
| naive 30 km/h              |     69.5 s |   **30.6 s** |     197 s |
| ratio of sums, city        |     71.3 s |       47.3 s |     171 s |
| least squares (before)     |     71.4 s |       58.2 s |     166 s |
| ratio of sums, per cell    |     73.5 s |       49.9 s |     177 s |

Least squares minimised the wrong thing: pickups are long-tailed — one driver
stuck behind a bridge — and a squared-error fit is dragged along by the tail.
Per-cell corrections made every variant worse. What won is fitted to absolute
error: `seconds = overhead + pace × metres` over the last 150 pickups, the pace
a median for each candidate overhead, the overhead the one that misses by the
least. City-wide, no cells.

## Result

Live, on the scarcity benchmark's run — 300 drivers doing 1.5 km trips, 2
requests a second, most around three hotspots — over 135 pickups:

|              | learned | naive 30 km/h |
| ------------ | ------: | ------------: |
| mean error   |  50.4 s |        53.5 s |
| p90 error    |   134 s |         159 s |
| median error |  33.8 s |        25.8 s |

And on the 176 usable pickups kept as a fixture, which a test replays on every
run: learned mean 63.1 s against 66.7 s, p90 172 s against 196 s.

**A modest improvement, where it counts most.** The badly wrong predictions —
the ones a rider remembers — are 16% less wrong. The typical prediction is
slightly worse than the naive one, and the test says so rather than asserting
otherwise.

The ceiling is low for a reason the model cannot see. Straight-line speed across
these pickups ran from 8.5 to 40 km/h between the tenth and ninetieth
percentiles, and most of that spread is one simulated driver being faster than
another — each has a fixed cruising speed. The next gains are in information,
not fitting: the driver's own recent speed, which every ping already carries,
and road distance rather than straight-line distance.

## Reproducing

```sh
SIM_TRIP_KM=1.5 SIM_HOTSPOT_SHARE=0.7 SIM_REQUESTS_PER_SECOND=2 make dev-sim
```

`surge_ingest_pickup_seconds` counts the pickups observed;
`surge_gateway_eta_error_seconds{model="learned"|"naive"}` is the running
comparison. A rider following a driver on `/ride` sees "arriving in about N min"
while the driver is on the way.
